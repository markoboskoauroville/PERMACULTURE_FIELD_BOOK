# The Patient Ground _a field book on permaculture_

Where it came from, what it actually says, what the evidence now shows, and what any of it means on limestone with four months of no rain.

Marko Boško · Mantra Productions · Zagreb

Permaculture has a problem that its enthusiasts rarely name: it is a design system with an unusually confident vocabulary and, for most of its life, almost no data. For forty years you could read a shelf of books telling you what a food forest yields and find that not one of them had measured it.

That changed recently, and it changed in permaculture's favour — which is exactly why the change is worth writing about carefully rather than triumphantly. This book takes the movement seriously enough to check it.

It also has a specific location in mind. Most permaculture writing in English assumes a temperate climate with rain distributed through the year. I am writing from Croatia, and the ground I care about is Dalmatian karst: limestone, thin soil, a hard bora wind, four rainless summer months, and fire. Almost every standard technique needs adjusting before it works here, and chapter nine is about how.

What this book is not

Not a design course, and not a substitute for standing on your own land through four seasons before you move any soil. The most important instruction in permaculture is _observe first_ , and no book can do that part for you.

Chapter one

## Tasmania, 1974

Mollison, Holmgren, and a word made out of two other words.

One idea, planted in poor soil, in a place nobody was watching.

The word is a compression of _permanent agriculture_ , later widened to _permanent culture_ once it became clear that the ideas were not only about farming.

**Bill Mollison** (1928–2016) was a Tasmanian with an unusual working life behind him — bushman, fisherman, trapper, then wildlife biologist and lecturer — and a settled fury about what industrial agriculture was doing to the land he had spent decades watching. **David Holmgren** (born 1955) was his student, methodical where Mollison was volcanic. Their collaboration in the mid-1970s produced _Permaculture One_ in 1978.

### What was new

Almost none of the individual techniques. Mulching, composting, contour planting, tree crops, mixed polyculture — all of it existed, much of it for centuries.

What was new was the framing: treat a farm or a garden as a **designed system** whose elements are placed according to their relationships rather than by convention. Where does the water go. What is uphill of what. Which element's output is another element's input. That is a systems-thinking move applied to land, and applied consistently it does produce different arrangements than habit does.

### Two temperaments, and a fork

Mollison's _Permaculture: A Designers' Manual_ (1988) remains the movement's central text — enormous, encyclopaedic, opinionated, and impatient with anybody who wanted evidence. He was openly hostile to academic agriculture, which had consequences discussed in chapter ten.

Holmgren went the other way. _Permaculture: Principles & Pathways Beyond Sustainability_ (2002) sets out the twelve principles most people now learn, and is more careful, more systemic and considerably less certain. If you read one, read Holmgren; if you want the encyclopaedia, Mollison.

Chapter two

## The ancestors

Smith, Howard, Yeomans, Fukuoka — most of it was already there.

Four people had already written most of it down. The word came later.

A movement that tells you to observe before acting should be able to look at its own genealogy. Permaculture's is well documented and is often skipped in courses, which does the students no favours — the ancestors are frequently clearer than their descendants.

### J. Russell Smith — _Tree Crops: A Permanent Agriculture_ (1929)

An American geographer who looked at eroding hillsides and argued that steep land should carry **trees** whose crops feed animals and people — chestnut, oak, honey locust, carob, mulberry — rather than annual grain. His subtitle is where the word _permaculture_ ultimately comes from. Almost everything the modern food forest claims is in this book, written half a century earlier.

### Sir Albert Howard — the Indore process (1930s–40s)

A British agricultural scientist working in India who systematised aerobic composting into a repeatable method, and argued in _An Agricultural Testament_ (1940) that soil fertility, plant health, animal health and human health form one chain. The organic movement descends from him directly. So does every compost heap turned on a schedule.

### P. A. Yeomans — keyline design (1950s)

An Australian mining engineer who worked out, from reading the shape of land, how to move water across a landscape rather than down it. His _keyline_ system identifies the point where a valley's slope changes and lays out cultivation and dams relative to it, so that runoff is spread from wet valleys toward dry ridges instead of concentrating and cutting.

Yeomans is the most technically rigorous ancestor and the most underused. If you have real slope, read him before you read anything about swales.

### Masanobu Fukuoka — _The One-Straw Revolution_ (1975)

A Japanese microbiologist turned farmer who arrived at what he called natural farming: no tillage, no fertiliser, no pesticide, no weeding, seed sown in clay pellets into standing cover. He is philosophically the opposite of Mollison — where permaculture designs, Fukuoka _subtracts_ , asking of every practice whether it could simply be stopped.

The honest caveat: his method was tuned over decades to one climate, one soil and one pair of hands. Attempts to transplant it directly have often failed, and that failure is instructive rather than embarrassing.

Why this matters

Permaculture's real contribution is **synthesis and transmission** — it took scattered work and made a teachable design language, and then a global network to teach it. That is a genuine achievement. It is a smaller and more defensible claim than the one sometimes made, and stating it accurately protects the movement from the charge that it renamed other people's work.

Chapter three

## Three ethics, twelve principles

Stated, then examined for what they actually commit you to.

Everything else in the system is an argument about what belongs in the hands.

### The three ethics

  1. **Earth care.**
  2. **People care.**
  3. **Fair share** — return of surplus, and limits to consumption.

The third is the one that has drifted. Mollison's original formulation was blunter and included setting limits to population and consumption; the softer "fair share" is a later gloss. The drift is worth noticing, because the harder version is a real constraint and the softer one can be satisfied by giving away courgettes.

### The twelve principles (Holmgren, 2002)

#| Principle| What it means on the ground  
---|---|---  
1| Observe and interact| A year of watching before earthworks. The most-cited and least-obeyed.  
2| Catch and store energy| Water, sunlight, biomass, fertility — hold it before it leaves.  
3| Obtain a yield| A system that feeds nobody is a hobby. Chapter ten takes this literally.  
4| Apply self-regulation and accept feedback| Notice failure early rather than defend the design.  
5| Use and value renewable resources| Prefer what regrows to what depletes.  
6| Produce no waste| Every output is somebody's input, or it is a problem.  
7| Design from patterns to details| Get the water and the layout right before choosing cultivars.  
8| Integrate rather than segregate| Placement is the design. Proximity does the work.  
9| Use small and slow solutions| The most reliably correct one. Small failures are survivable.  
10| Use and value diversity| Insurance, not decoration.  
11| Use edges, value the marginal| Boundaries are the most productive zones in most ecosystems.  
12| Creatively use and respond to change| The design is a hypothesis, not a plan.  
  
The honest criticism of the principles

They are broad enough to justify almost any decision after the fact. "Use small and slow solutions" and "creatively respond to change" can each be invoked to defend opposite choices, which makes them poor at settling arguments — and settling arguments is what a design framework is for. They work best as a **checklist for noticing what you have forgotten** , and worst as a rulebook. Used the second way they become a vocabulary for justifying what you were going to do anyway.

Chapter four

## Zones and sectors

The one design tool that survives contact with a real site.

Rings around the door. Everything you visit daily belongs in the first one.

If you throw out everything else, keep this. Zoning is permaculture's most durable idea, it is verifiable within a season, and it is the thing most new gardeners get wrong.

### Zones — organised by how often you go there

  * **Zone 0** — the house.
  * **Zone 1** — visited daily, often more than once. Herbs, salad, the things you pick with wet hands while cooking. Small, intensive, and immediately outside the door.
  * **Zone 2** — visited most days. Vegetable beds, soft fruit, chickens.
  * **Zone 3** — weekly. Main crops, orchard, larger livestock.
  * **Zone 4** — occasional. Woodlot, forage, timber, grazing.
  * **Zone 5** — unmanaged. Left alone deliberately, as reference and habitat.

The failure mode is universal and worth stating plainly: **people put the herb bed at the bottom of the garden.** It is a lovely spot. Nobody walks there in the rain, the herbs go to seed unpicked, and within two years the bed is a weed patch and the gardener concludes they are bad at gardening. The bed was in the wrong zone. That is all.

Zone 5 also deserves defending, because it is the first thing cut when land feels short. It is your control sample — the only way to know what your climate does without you.

### Sectors — energies arriving from outside

Zones are about your movement; sectors are about what comes at the site regardless of you. Sun angles in summer and winter. Prevailing wind, and the damaging one, which is often a different direction. Water arriving from uphill. Frost pooling in hollows. Fire approach. Noise, dust, the neighbour's spray drift. View.

You draw them as wedges radiating across the site, and then you place things to block, filter, channel or admit each one. In Dalmatia the bora sector alone determines most of a design, and getting it wrong is not a matter of reduced yield — it is a matter of losing young trees outright.

Chapter five

## Water first

Swales, keyline, ponds — and when a swale is the wrong answer.

Slow it, spread it, sink it. In that order, and before anything else.

Water is the first design decision on any site, and it is upstream of everything — soil, planting, layout, buildings. Get it wrong and you spend a decade compensating.

### The rule

**Slow it. Spread it. Sink it.** Water leaving your land fast is carrying your soil with it and taking your fertility to somebody else's river.

### Swales

A swale is a ditch dug on contour — level along its whole length — with the spoil mounded on the downhill side. Runoff collects in it, sits, and soaks in rather than running off. Trees are planted on the mound.

They are the most photographed technique in permaculture and the most misapplied. A swale is right when you have **moderate slope, enough soil depth to hold water, and periodic heavy rain**.

When a swale is wrong

  * **On clay with poor drainage** — you have built a pond you did not want, and drowned the tree roots on the mound.
  * **On steep slopes** — a saturated swale on a steep hillside is a landslide waiting for the right storm. This has killed people.
  * **On karst** , which is my case and possibly yours. Limestone is already a drainage system. Water put into a swale on thin soil over fissured rock disappears vertically into the aquifer instead of moving laterally into the root zone. You have done earthworks to achieve nothing.
  * **Where the soil is already too wet** — the technique is for retaining water, not for having more of it.

### Keyline, which is usually the better answer on slope

Yeomans's system works from the **keypoint** — the place in a valley where the steep upper slope flattens. Ploughing on lines that run slightly _off_ contour from there moves water outward from the wet valley toward the dry ridges, drought-proofing the whole face rather than storing water in one line. It is subtler than swales, needs a decent read of the land, and does far less damage when you get it slightly wrong.

### What actually works on karst

Since the fissures win, the strategy is to stop treating the rock as storage and use these instead:

  * **Roof catchment into cisterns.** The traditional Dalmatian answer for two thousand years, and still the best one. It is not romantic and it is extremely effective.
  * **Terraces behind dry stone walls** — the local _suhozid_ — which hold soil in place and let it accumulate depth.
  * **Heavy mulch and shade** , so that the water that does arrive is not lost to evaporation within hours.
  * **Small individual basins** around each tree rather than long linear earthworks.
  * **Deep-rooting, drought-adapted species** chosen so the plant solves the problem instead of the engineering.

Chapter six

## Soil

The food web, compost, mulch, no-dig, and what the trials show.

Not a substrate. A population, most of which nobody has named.

Soil is where permaculture's claims are strongest and where the mainstream evidence is most supportive, so this chapter can be less hedged than most.

### The living soil

A teaspoon of healthy soil contains an enormous population of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes and arthropods. The relationship that matters most for a grower is **mycorrhizal** : fungi colonise plant roots, extend the effective root system far beyond what the plant could build alone, and trade water and phosphorus for sugars. This is well established, not fringe, and it explains most of what follows.

Tillage tears that network apart. Soluble fertiliser makes the plant stop paying for it. Both produce a plant dependent on the grower.

### The practices, ranked by how solid the evidence is

  * **Keep the soil covered.** Bare soil erodes, bakes, loses moisture and loses carbon. Mulch or living cover. This is not controversial anywhere.
  * **Compost.** Aerobic, turned, a rough balance of carbon-rich and nitrogen-rich material, kept damp. Howard's Indore process in one line.
  * **Reduce tillage.** No-dig and reduced-till systems show real benefits for structure, soil biology and carbon retention. The honest note: weed pressure can rise, and mulch-heavy no-dig depends on a supply of organic matter that has to come from somewhere.
  * **Living roots year-round.** Cover crops feed the soil biology when the main crop is absent.
  * **Chop and drop.** Cut growth and leave it where it fell. Free, effective, and requires no infrastructure.

Where to be careful

Soil carbon sequestration claims in the movement often run well ahead of the measurements. Building soil organic matter is real and valuable — for water-holding capacity, structure and fertility. Whether a given practice sequesters a given tonnage of carbon per hectare per year, permanently, is a much harder question with contested answers. Claim the agronomy, which is solid. Be careful with the climate arithmetic, which is not.

Chapter seven

## The food forest

Seven layers, succession, and the yields nobody measured.

Seven storeys, if you count them generously. Most people build two and call it seven.

The food forest is permaculture's flagship image and its most oversold product. Both halves of that sentence are true and a good practitioner holds them together.

### The seven layers

  1. **Canopy** — full-size trees. Walnut, chestnut, carob.
  2. **Sub-canopy** — dwarf and small fruit. Fig, pomegranate, almond, olive.
  3. **Shrub** — currants, berries, hazel, rosemary at scale.
  4. **Herbaceous** — perennial vegetables and herbs.
  5. **Ground cover** — spreading, living mulch.
  6. **Root** — the layer below everything else.
  7. **Vine** — climbing the structure. Grape, kiwi.

Some versions add mycelium as an eighth, which is reasonable and slightly cheeky.

### The guild claim

A _guild_ is a group of plants asserted to support one another — a fruit tree with nitrogen fixers, dynamic accumulators, pest-confusing aromatics and insectary flowers around it. Some of this rests on solid ground: nitrogen fixation is real chemistry, and flowering plants genuinely bring in predatory insects.

But the specific published guild recipes are largely **untested folklore** , repeated from book to book without anybody running a trial. The best-known example is the "dynamic accumulator" — the claim that comfrey and certain others mine specific minerals from deep soil and concentrate them in their leaves. The mechanism is plausible. The specific mineral tables circulating in permaculture literature trace back to very thin sources and, where anyone has checked, have not held up well. Comfrey is still an excellent mulch plant. It is just not a mineral pump with a known specification.

### Time, honestly

Years one to three look like failure. Years three to seven, the shrubs and small fruit start paying. Years seven to fifteen, the canopy comes in and the system starts behaving like a system. There is no version of this that produces a serious yield in season two, and anybody selling one is selling something.

### The Mediterranean version

Temperate food-forest design assumes shade is the limiting factor. Here **water** is, and shade is an asset — so the structure inverts. Canopy exists to shade the ground and cut evaporation. Spacing is wider. Species are the ones that have been doing this here for millennia: olive, fig, almond, carob, pomegranate, grape, with rosemary, sage, lavender and thyme underneath. This is not an exotic import. It is what a Dalmatian smallholding already looked like a hundred years ago, and the honest framing is that permaculture here mostly means _rebuilding what was abandoned_.

Chapter eight

## Animals in the system

Chickens as tractors, and the limits of the metaphor.

The most efficient element on the site, and the only one that needs you every single day.

Animals close loops that plants cannot. They also do not take holidays, and the second fact is the one designs underestimate.

### What each one is for

  * **Chickens** — scratch, eat pests and weed seeds, and deposit concentrated nitrogen. Moved in a run over a bed, they clear and fertilise it. The "chicken tractor" is a real technique, not a slogan.
  * **Ducks** — better than chickens on slugs, worse on soil, and they need water.
  * **Sheep and goats** — grazing and fire management. On Mediterranean scrub, goats are the traditional and effective answer, and also the reason much of the coast is bare rock. Fencing is not optional.
  * **Pigs** — powerful ground-clearing, but they will rearrange a site completely, and in a food forest that is destruction rather than management.
  * **Bees** — pollination plus a yield, and the single best diagnostic instrument on any site. When the bees struggle, something is wrong before you can see it.

### The honest arithmetic

Animals need feeding and watering every day of the year, including when you are ill, travelling or working a Nova TV shift that ends at two in the morning. The design question is not whether animals close loops — they do — but whether you or somebody reliable is physically present daily. If the answer is no, the correct design has no animals in it, and that is a competent decision rather than a failure of ambition.

Chapter nine

## Karst, bora and fire

Designing for the Mediterranean, where the standard advice breaks.

Built without mortar so the wind goes through it instead of taking it down.

Nearly all permaculture writing in English is temperate. Applied unmodified to Dalmatian karst it produces expensive failure, and the failures are predictable enough to list.

### The four conditions

#### Karst

Limestone, fissured, with thin discontinuous soil in pockets. Water does not run across it; it disappears into it. Consequences: swales are usually pointless (chapter five), soil must be _built and held_ rather than improved in place, and terracing is the fundamental move.

#### Bora

The dry, cold, violent downslope wind off the mountains, in gusts that shred foliage and snap young stems. It also strips moisture at a rate that surprises people from wetter countries.

The answer is windbreaks, and the counterintuitive part is that a **permeable** windbreak works better than a solid one. A wall creates violent turbulence on its lee side; a shelterbelt that lets roughly half the wind through slows it for a distance of many times its own height. This is why _suhozid_ dry stone walls — unmortared, with gaps — are the correct traditional technology and not merely the picturesque one. They are on the UNESCO intangible heritage list, and they are engineering.

#### Summer drought

Effectively no rain for three to four months, in the hottest part of the year. Every planting decision follows from this. Autumn planting, not spring, so roots establish over the wet season. Heavy mulch. Shade as an asset. Cisterns. And species that expect it.

#### Fire

The condition most often left out of permaculture design entirely, and on this coast it is not optional. A dense mulched food forest full of resinous Mediterranean aromatics is, in August, fuel. Design implications: defensible space around structures, green firebreaks of low-flammability species, grazing or cutting to keep fuel loads down, water storage that is accessible for firefighting, and access tracks wide enough for a vehicle.

The Dalmatian species list

Olive, fig, almond, carob, pomegranate, grape, caper. Rosemary, sage, lavender, thyme, immortelle. Windbreaks of cypress or holm oak. This is not a designed polyculture invented by anyone — it is what survived here, selected over millennia by exactly the four conditions above. Start from it. Then experiment at the edges, on a small scale, and expect to lose some of the experiments.

Chapter ten

## Does it actually work?

Forty years without data, and what the last decade found.

The question the movement avoided for forty years, and then mostly passed.

This chapter is why the book exists. Every other permaculture text will tell you the system works. Very few will tell you how anybody knows.

### The problem, stated fairly

Mollison was openly hostile to academic agriculture, and the movement inherited that stance. The result was a decades-long separation: permaculture developed inside courses, books and demonstration sites rather than inside trials. Claims propagated by repetition. **Ferguson and Lovell** , in a 2013 review in _Agronomy for Sustainable Development_ , documented this isolation from science directly — and also made an observation that stings: permaculture's techniques largely overlap with mainstream agroecology, sometimes with a distinctive vocabulary laid over them. _Food forest_ is agroforestry. _Guild_ is synergistic polyculture.

### What has been found since

  * **The principles have support.** A 2018 review in _Sustainability_ by Krebs and Bach went through all twelve of Holmgren's principles and found scientific evidence for each — largely because they overlap heavily with agroecological principles that have been studied for decades. Note carefully what this does and does not show: the principles are sound; it says nothing about whether a particular garden produces food.
  * **And now there is yield data.** **Reiff et al. (2024)** produced the first large-scale empirical study of permaculture production in Central Europe: eleven commercial permaculture sites, mostly in Germany, plus Switzerland and Luxembourg. Using the Land Equivalent Ratio to compare mixed cropping against monoculture averages, they found food production **on average comparable to conventional and organic agriculture**.

How to hold that result

It is genuinely good news and it is not a vindication of everything. Eleven sites is not many. They were commercial operations, therefore self-selected for competence. Variability between sites was considerable — which the authors flagged themselves, carefully. And "comparable to conventional" is a real finding precisely because it is modest: it does not support the claim, common in the movement, that these systems dramatically outproduce conventional farming.

What it does support: a well-run permaculture site can feed people at a rate in the same range as the alternatives, while doing better on soil, water and biodiversity. That is a strong enough claim. It does not need inflating.

### The criticisms that still stand

  * **Untested guild recipes** circulating as though established. Chapter seven.
  * **Labour is rarely counted.** Yield per hectare is not the only number; yield per hour of human work matters enormously to whether anybody can actually live this way, and it is seldom reported.
  * **The certificate economy.** The two-week Permaculture Design Certificate is the movement's main institution and its main revenue. There is no accreditation body, no examination worth the name, and a graduate may have designed nothing. Some PDC teachers are excellent. The credential itself guarantees nothing.
  * **Demonstration-site bias.** The photographed sites are the successes, often supported by course income rather than by production. The failures are not written up, which is precisely the information a beginner most needs.

None of this makes permaculture wrong. It makes it a design framework with good principles, real techniques mostly borrowed from elsewhere, one decent body of yield evidence, and a culture that has historically been better at teaching than at measuring. Which is a fair description, and more use than a slogan.

Chapter eleven

## Kukljica EcoOasis

One degraded plot on Otok Ugljan, and the design questions it poses.

Thin soil, hard rock, four dry months, and a view worth the trouble.

Kukljica EcoOasis is a land restoration project on **Otok Ugljan** , in the Zadar archipelago, which I am developing with **Toni Franović**. It is the reason the previous two chapters are written the way they are.

### The conditions, in outline

An Adriatic island plot carries every constraint in chapter nine at once. Karst limestone with thin pocket soil. Full exposure to bora from the northeast. A rainless summer at the hottest time of year. Real fire risk in August. And — the condition specific to the islands — the whole landscape is **post-abandonment**. These terraces were farmed for centuries and then left when the population moved to the mainland and to tourism. What looks like wilderness is usually collapsed agriculture.

That last point changes the project's nature. This is not designing a system onto blank land. It is **archaeology followed by repair** : finding the old terrace lines, rebuilding the walls, identifying which of the surviving trees are abandoned cultivars rather than volunteers, and working out what the previous three generations knew before deciding what to add.

### The design questions, in order

  1. **Water.** Catchment area, cistern capacity, and whether the traditional roof-to-cistern system can carry the intended planting. On karst this is settled first or not at all.
  2. **The walls.** Which terraces still stand, which can be rebuilt, and in what order. Soil depth follows from this.
  3. **Wind.** Where the bora actually lands on this particular slope, and where permeable shelter goes.
  4. **Fire.** Defensible space, access, and fuel management as a standing commitment rather than a note.
  5. **What is already there.** A full inventory of surviving olives, figs and vines before anything is planted or cleared.
  6. **Presence.** How many days a month somebody is actually on site. This determines whether animals, irrigation and annual vegetables belong in the design at all, and it is the question most likely to be answered optimistically.

What is not in this chapter

Site measurements. Plot size, aspect, exact rainfall, cistern volume, tree inventory, soil depth, the existing terrace survey. I have written the design _questions_ because those are general; I have not invented the answers, and the numbers a real design needs are the ones Toni and I have on the ground rather than the ones I could make sound plausible here. When the survey exists, this chapter gets rewritten with figures in it. Until then it is a framework, and it says so.

Chapter twelve

## The first year

What to do, in order, on a piece of land you have just got.

The most productive tool on the site, for the first twelve months.

### Months 1 to 12: observe

Do not move soil. Watch instead, and write it down.

  * Where water arrives, pools and leaves, during the heaviest rain of the year.
  * Where the wind comes from, and which direction does the damage — usually two different answers.
  * Sun and shade at midsummer and midwinter. They are not the same site.
  * Where frost sits, if it does. Where snow lies longest.
  * What already grows, including the weeds, which are reading the soil for you.
  * What the neighbours grow, and what their grandparents grew. On an abandoned Mediterranean landscape this is the highest-value data available and it costs a coffee.

### Then, in this order

  1. **Access.** Paths and tracks first, because everything else is built along them and moving them later is expensive.
  2. **Water.** Catchment, storage, and the shape of the land. Before planting anything permanent.
  3. **Shelter.** Windbreaks take years to work, so they are planted early even though they yield nothing.
  4. **Soil, in Zone 1 only.** Build a small area properly rather than a large area badly.
  5. **Trees.** The longest lead time, so once water and shelter are settled, they go in.
  6. **Everything else.** Annual beds, animals, structures — all of which can be moved, unlike the five items above.

The one rule

**Small and slow.** Of the twelve principles it is the one that is unambiguously right, and it is the one enthusiasm most reliably overrides. Half a hectare planted well and observed carefully teaches more in three years than five hectares planted in a burst of energy and then abandoned in year two — and the second outcome is by far the more common one.

## What I could not verify

Stated as plainly as what I could.

  * **I read the Reiff et al. study through its abstract and through published commentary, not in full.** The headline finding — production comparable to conventional and organic across eleven sites — is reported accurately, but I have not examined their methods, and Land Equivalent Ratio comparisons involve choices that a specialist would want to inspect.
  * **The Krebs and Bach review** is likewise summarised from its abstract and conclusions.
  * **No numbers for Kukljica.** Chapter eleven deliberately contains no plot size, rainfall figure, cistern volume, soil depth or tree count, because the survey is not done and I will not invent site data. That chapter is a framework and says so inside itself.
  * **The dynamic accumulator critique** reflects my reading of where those mineral tables came from. I have not traced every citation to its origin, and somebody who has may be able to rescue part of the claim.
  * **Soil carbon sequestration** is flagged as contested rather than resolved, because I am not competent to resolve it and the literature does not agree.
  * **Fire design** is given in general terms. Actual defensible-space requirements are a matter of local regulation and local fire service advice, and you should get both rather than take a book's word.
  * **Swale failure modes** are described from the general literature and from the physics. The landslide risk on steep saturated slopes is real and documented, but I have not cited specific incidents.
  * **I am not a professional grower.** I have a project, an interest and a habit of checking claims. Anything in chapters five, six and seven should be tested against somebody who farms your specific climate.
